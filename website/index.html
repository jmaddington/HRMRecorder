Who it's for

A small app for a small audience.

HRM Recorder isn't a fitness app. It doesn't track workouts, give you scores, or nag you. It records heart data, faithfully and continuously, for the people who actually need that.

Living with POTS, tachycardia, or dysautonomia

Symptoms that don't show up on demand in a 20-minute appointment. The standing spikes, the racing episodes at 3 a.m., the slow drifts between clinic visits — captured as they happen, in data your doctor can actually look at.

Health-curious humans

Resting heart rate over months. How your sleep affects your morning HR. The slow climb you noticed after a viral illness. Your data, sampled continuously, in a format you can actually look at.

Athletes tracking recovery

R-R intervals out of the box, sample-level. Feed them into your HRV tool of choice — Kubios, custom scripts, whatever you trust. No summary-only black box. No watch-vendor lock-in.

Where it fits

Between a watch and a Holter.

A watch is easy but coarse. A Holter sees everything — but you get it for a day or two, and symptoms don't schedule themselves around appointments. A chest strap with HRM Recorder sits in between: months of beat-by-beat heart-rate data for the price of the strap and a couple of coin cells.

Features

Everything that matters. Nothing else.

The app does two things: record heart rate, and let you export it. Every feature below either makes one of those two things more reliable, or lets you see what you've recorded.

Standard BLE straps

Works with any sensor exposing the standard Heart Rate Service. Garmin HRM-Pro+, Polar H10, Wahoo TICKR, COROS, and more.

R-R intervals captured

Every R-R interval the strap reports is written to your database — the data HRV analysis actually needs.

Background recording

Keeps recording with the screen off and the app backgrounded. If iOS suspends the app, it resumes the session when your strap reconnects.

Live Activity

Glance at live BPM from your Lock Screen and Dynamic Island, no need to open the app. (iOS 16.1+)

History graphs

Min–max band and average line, from the last hour out to 30 days, plus a graph for every session. Pinch to zoom. See it below.

Multi-strap aware

Swap straps mid-session, or record from two at once. Each sample is attributed to the strap that produced it.

Real CSV export

One row per strap reading, about one per second — timestamp, BPM, R-R intervals, sensor identity. RFC-4180 quoted. Opens cleanly in Excel, Numbers, pandas, anything.

Local SQLite

Data lives in a single SQLite file in the app sandbox. WAL mode for durable real-time writes. Schema is published — bring your own tools.

Battery aware

Shows your strap's battery level and warns when it's running low — so you don't lose hours of data to a flat coin cell.

Optional self-hosted sync

Want a backup? Point it at your own server. No service to sign up for; no third party between you and your data. Open protocol — documented here.

Graphs

A month of heartbeats, on one screen.

Every recording builds the picture: a shaded band from the lowest to the highest reading in each time bucket, with a line through the averages. Step back to 30 days or zoom into the last hour, pinch and scroll, or open any single session at full ~1 Hz detail. Where you weren't recording, the line simply breaks — gaps stay gaps. FAQ

Questions you might have.

If yours isn't here, email jonathan@jonathanaddington.com.

What straps does it work with?
Any Bluetooth strap that exposes the standard Heart Rate Service (UUID 0x180D). That includes Garmin HRM-Pro+, Polar H10 and H9, Wahoo TICKR family, COROS HR monitors, and most modern chest straps. Optical arm bands that speak BLE HR (like the Polar OH1 / Verity Sense) also work. Apple Watch and other watches are not supported — the strap connects directly to your iPhone.
Why doesn't it record GPS or activities?
On purpose. Activity tracking is the most-served corner of the iOS app market — there are dozens of excellent options. Continuous, raw heart data with R-R intervals is not. HRM Recorder stays single-purpose so it can be reliable, small, and stay running in the background for as long as you need it.
How do I get HRV out of this?
Export your session as CSV. The rr_ms column has every R-R interval the strap reported, in milliseconds, semicolon-joined per packet. Drop that into Kubios, the hrv-analysis Python package, your own R notebook, whatever you trust. The app stores the raw data; HRV math is up to you and your tools.
What iOS version do I need?
iOS 16.0 or later. Works on every iPhone that supports iOS 16, including older models — the app is intentionally lightweight so it runs comfortably on a 5-year-old phone. The Lock Screen Live Activity needs iOS 16.1; everything else works on 16.0.
What happens if my phone restarts or the strap disconnects?
If your strap drops out of range, the app keeps trying to reconnect and resumes the active session automatically — no samples lost once the strap comes back. If iOS relaunches the app in the background on a strap event after killing it, the session you were recording is resumed under the same ID.
Can I record from two straps at once?
Yes. Samples are attributed to the strap that produced them, and the CSV export includes per-row sensor identity. Useful for comparing a chest strap against an arm band, or recording two people at the same time.
Is my data sent anywhere?
Not unless you choose to set up self-hosted sync to your own server. By default, everything stays on your phone in a SQLite file inside the app's sandbox. CSV export is user-initiated — the file is handed to the iOS share sheet, and where it goes from there is up to you.
Is this medical-grade?
No. HRM Recorder is not a medical device and isn't intended to diagnose, treat, or monitor any condition. It records what your Bluetooth strap reports. It's worth knowing what that is, though: a chest strap reads the heart's electrical signal — the same signal an ECG reads — and chest-strap R-R timing has been validated closely against clinical ECG Holters. What it records is heart rate, beat by beat — not rhythm. It cannot see the waveform shapes a cardiologist uses to diagnose arrhythmias. Bring the data to your clinician; let them interpret it.
Why isn't there a hosted sync service or sharing site?
Because hosting other people's health data is a heavy legal and ethical obligation — GDPR and similar laws apply even to a free service run in good faith. Rather than do that badly, HRM Recorder ships an open sync protocol and the tools to run your own receiver: a documented API, a reference server, and a small Flask example you can self-host. Someday a proper sharing service might exist. Today, you get the tools.
How much does it cost?
Nothing. HRM Recorder is free and open source — the full source code lives at github.com/jmaddington/HRMRecorder. Read it, build it yourself, fork it, do what you want.
Who writes the code?
Mostly Claude, Anthropic's AI. A human developer designs the app, directs the work, reviews the code, and tests it on real hardware — but the large majority of the code is written by Claude. That's said plainly here because you deserve to know how the software you run is made, and because the entire history is public in the repository.
Can I contribute?
Yes — pull requests are welcome, and so are bug reports and feature ideas via GitHub issues. One honest caveat: the maintainer has a more-than-full-time day job, so responses can be slow. Slow doesn't mean ignored.
